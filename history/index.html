History

The James John Trust

How the founder of St. Johns left everything he owned to give St. Johns children free public schools, and what became of his gift.

Last updated November 2, 2026

A gift to St. Johns children

The land James John claimed is the traditional homeland of Chinookan peoples, including the Multnomah and Clackamas, along with other Native nations who lived, fished, and traded along the Willamette and Columbia rivers for thousands of years. His claim was made under the federal Donation Land Act of 1850, which granted Native land to white settlers, often before treaties had been made with the tribes who lived there. We acknowledge that history as part of this story.

James John settled on the Willamette in 1846, filed a 320-acre donation land claim on May 1, 1850, and laid out the town himself, which he originally named “St. John.” In 1852, he started a ferry across the Willamette where the St. Johns Bridge stands today. He lived as a bachelor in St. Johns and left no direct heirs. When he died on May 28, 1886, he left his entire estate in trust for free public schools in St. Johns.

“A permanent and perpetual educational fund, to be forever used in promoting education.”
James John’s will, signed March 14, 1883

His will directed that his land be leased and then sold, with the money used to build schools and pay teachers. One block, Block 29 of James Johns’ Addition, was set aside for school buildings. The schools were to be free and “at all times open to children of the school district, which shall embrace the town of St. Johns.” According to historian David Overby, the money was to form “an irreducible fund,” with only the interest spent. The will also stated that buildings paid for with estate money “shall belong to my estate, and not to the district.”

His gravestone in the old Columbian Cemetery, placed in 1909 by the directors of St. Johns School District No. 2, reads simply: “James John – Friend of Education.”

Sources: James John’s will, quoted in John v. Smith, 91 F. 827 (D. Or. 1899); Overby (1966), citing the Sunday Oregonian, March 29, 1903, and January 22, 1961.

The courts upheld it twice

James John’s relatives challenged the will, claiming he was not of sound mind. The Oregon Supreme Court upheld it on December 21, 1896, and a federal court upheld it again on February 4, 1899, describing a trust for “a specific object” that is “clearly declared, and it is absolute.”

Sources: In re John’s Will, 47 P. 341 (Or. 1896); John v. Smith, 91 F. 827 (D. Or. 1899).

Most of the estate was lost early

Defending the will was costly: the Oregonian reported in 1907 that 20 acres were sold to pay for the litigation. Administrator Robert Catlin, who took over in 1901, was repeatedly accused by St. Johns residents of selling land far below its value and letting the property go to waste. After all the land sales, the trust held $14,515.98. Catlin then requested $9,070.39 in fees, leaving $5,445.59.

Writing in 1966, Overby concluded that “no real ‘education fund’ growth took place” and that the administrators “forgot or never knew their duties.”

Source: Overby (1966), citing Multnomah County Probate File No. 1268 and the Oregonian.

The trust’s school

The trust helped build James John High School on Block 29, on Syracuse Street between Philadelphia and Alta, across from the old St. Johns City Hall at what is now the east end of the St. Johns Bridge. It was completed in 1910, paid for with $7,768.32 from the trust plus local bonds. After high school students moved to Roosevelt in 1923, St. Johns grade school children used the building until today’s James John School opened.

A 1907 Oregonian article described Block 29 as “reserved by the will in perpetuity for the site of a schoolhouse.” When the city proposed reusing the empty building for its fire and police departments around 1930, “legal stipulations precluded the school from use for anything but educational purposes” (Nelson). The vacant building burned in an arson fire on September 22, 1934. Afterward, the school board’s clerk told the Oregonian that “the grant of the property provided it should only be used as a school.”

Sources: Donald R. Nelson, Pictorial History of St. Johns, pp. 14–15; Sunday Oregonian, September 1934; Overby (1966); 1922 Tumalum yearbook.

Portland Public Schools becomes trustee

St. Johns was its own city from 1903 until it voted to join Portland, which took effect on July 8, 1915. After that, its school district became part of Portland Public Schools. The trust kept its own trustees, but over time one of them, William F. Woodward, was also a Portland school board director. With the high school building vacant, the district sued to have the trust interpreted so it could sell the trust’s property, and asked that the trust be turned over to the district. A court ruled in the board’s favor in May 1932.

“Only for the benefit of the common school children in the original district of St. Johns, carrying out the terms of the will as closely as possible.”
St. Johns Review, December 1932, describing the school board’s use of the fund

Sources: Oregon Journal, December 27, 1932 (via Overby); “Formal Transfer of Jas. John Trust,” St. Johns Review, December 1932.

Block 29 today

Block 29, the land James John reserved for a school, was later sold. It is today the Bridgecrest Apartments at 7002–7140 N Alta Ave (legal description: James Johns Add, Block 29), which the St. Johns Heritage Association reports opened in 1971. County sales records only go back to 1986, so the date and terms of the district’s original sale have not yet been confirmed.

By 1966, Overby wrote, St. Johns schools were “supported solely by the taxpayer’s money, not St. John’s ‘perpetual educational fund.’”

Sources: Multnomah County property records (Property ID R191934); Nelson, Pictorial History of St. Johns, p. 15; Overby (1966).

Our view: how James John’s trust was failed

The history above is drawn from court records, newspapers, and local histories. What follows is our opinion, as St. Johns parents, neighbors, and business owners.

1. The school district should not have become trustee

James John placed his fund with independent trustees chosen by judges, who were to fill any vacancies themselves, and he wrote that buildings paid for with his money “shall belong to my estate, and not to the district.” The will let the trustees work “in concert with the directors of said school district,” as partners, not owners. A court allowed the school board to take over the trust in 1932, but in our view, placing the trust in the hands of the district it was meant to stand apart from went against his clear intent.

Sources: James John’s will, quoted in John v. Smith, 91 F. 827 (D. Or. 1899); Overby (1966).

2. Block 29 should have stayed in the service of St. Johns children

The will set Block 29 apart from all of James John’s other land, which was to be sold, and reserved it for school buildings. In 1907, the Oregonian described it as “reserved by the will in perpetuity for the site of a schoolhouse.” Around 1930, “legal stipulations precluded the school from use for anything but educational purposes,” and in 1934 the school board’s own clerk said “the grant of the property provided it should only be used as a school.” After the building burned, the district sold the land, which a court had allowed it to do in 1932, and Nelson notes the property could be sold as long as the money was used for education. We believe the one block James John set aside for St. Johns children should have remained theirs.

Sources: Overby (1966), citing the Oregonian, August 29, 1907; Nelson, Pictorial History of St. Johns, p. 15; Sunday Oregonian, September 1934.

3. The perpetual fund was never built

James John asked for “a permanent and perpetual educational fund,” and, according to Overby, an “irreducible fund” whose interest alone would be spent. Most of his estate was lost to lawsuits and administrators’ fees before the district was involved. But once it became trustee, the district held cash, Liberty bonds, and Block 29. We believe those assets, including the money from selling the land, should have been invested safely and kept as the permanent fund he asked for, so its earnings could still benefit St. Johns children today. Instead, by 1966, Overby wrote, St. Johns schools were supported “solely by the taxpayer’s money.”

Sources: James John’s will, quoted in John v. Smith (1899); Overby (1966); Oregon Journal, December 27, 1932.

We recognize that a court approved the district’s role as trustee, that the fund was small by the time the district took it over, and that we have not yet seen the district’s own records of how it was managed. That’s why we’re asking PPS for a full accounting.

Today’s James John School

Today’s James John School, at 7439 N Charleston Ave, sits on land the St. Johns school district bought in 1894 and 1926. It is not trust property; Overby called it “the school building named for him but in no way supported by him.” But children have gone to school on this site for more than 130 years. Central School, built in 1894 and later renamed Richard Williams School, housed St. Johns’ first high school classes and stood on today’s playground until it burned on January 13, 1930. Today’s building was completed in 1929, opened in January 1930, about two weeks after Central School burned, was named for the founder, and was found eligible for the National Register of Historic Places in PPS’s own 2009 historic assessment.

Portland Public Schools has proposed closing it.

Sources: PPS Historic Building Assessment (2009); Nelson, Pictorial History of St. Johns, p. 14.

Open questions for PPS

  • When was Block 29 sold, to whom, and for how much?
  • What happened to the money from that sale?
  • Did a court approve moving the trust fund into the general fund in 1949, and how much was in it?
  • How has the trust been used since 1932, and was that use consistent with the will’s purpose of serving St. Johns schoolchildren?
  • Is any portion of the James John trust still held by PPS?
